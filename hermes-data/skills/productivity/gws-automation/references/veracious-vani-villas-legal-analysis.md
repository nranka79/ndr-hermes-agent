## Key Legal Issues

### 1. Are Landowners Bound by the Mortgage?
**Yes, under Indian law — with limited exceptions.**

The landowners signed the mortgage deed as "co-obligors" in March 2016 alongside the developer. Under the DTD, they expressly agreed to be jointly and severally liable. Key legal provisions:
- **TPA Section 58:** Definition of mortgage — any person having an interest in the property can mortgage it
- **Co-obligor liability:** The JDA (Nov 2010) between Veracious and G.V. Veerasetty contemplated financing, and the landowners' subsequent signature on the mortgage documents was consistent with that understanding
- **Landowner defenses available:**
  1. Fraud / misrepresentation in getting them to sign (must prove)
  2. Unconscionable terms (very high bar in commercial transactions)
  3. The mortgage covers MORE than their share (128 units include both developer and landowner portions — argue over-encumbrance)
  4. Procedural: notice requirements under DTD not followed before enforcement

### 2. Can Civil Suit Proceed During IBC Liquidation?
**Contested — depends on secured creditor status.**

The ₹55.30 Cr NCD is a secured debt (128 apartments mortgaged). Secured creditors generally can enforce their security during liquidation. However:
- IBC Section 14 moratorium stays all proceedings against the corporate debtor
- The civil suit is against both the company AND the guarantors/promoters
- The Personal Guarantees (separate from the corporate NCD) may allow claims against guarantors even during liquidation

### 3. Home Buyer / Allottee Rights vs. Mortgagee Rights
**Registered mortgage generally has priority over allottees.**

However:
- If any flats were sold to allottees BEFORE the mortgage (unlikely — construction started after 2016)
- RERA registration gives allottees statutory protection
- The interim order (Feb 2019) already restrains creation of 3rd party rights — this includes pre-sold allottees without NOC

## Legal Analysis Summary

### Landowner's Strongest Arguments
1. **Over-encumbrance:** 128 units mortgaged includes both developer share and landowner share — argue that landowner units were not meant to secure the full ₹55.30 Cr
2. **Procedural lapses:** Did the Debenture Trustee follow the exact notice procedure under DTD Clause 17 before declaring default?
3. **Forceful possession:** The landowners claim they were forcibly taken physical possession of their apartments — if this is documented, it could support a criminal complaint or argue that the security was improperly obtained
4. **NCLT moratorium:** Any enforcement action against the company must navigate the IBC moratorium

### Lender's Strong Arguments
1. Landowners signed as co-obligors — very hard to escape liability
2. Registered mortgage deed is binding and enforceable
3. Security is clearly defined (128 units, specific flat numbers)
4. Default is documented (demand notices served 2017-2018, no response)
5. Interim injunction already granted (Feb 2019) restraining alienation

### Risk Assessment
- **Landowner probability of success (escaping mortgage liability):** ~20–30%
- **Lender probability of full recovery:** ~60–70% (secured, but timeline 4-6 years)
- **Key variables:** quality of notice procedure, whether any flats were sold without NOC, NCLT's treatment of simultaneous proceedings

## Known Risks / Issues

1. **Forceful possession by landowners:** Documented in case records — landowners claim they physically re-occupied mortgaged flats. May have criminal dimensions.
2. **₹13.21 Cr fraudulent trading award:** Directors found personally liable under IBC Section 66 — but recovery uncertain (limited personal assets).
3. **Parallel proceedings risk:** Both civil suit and NCLT liquidation running simultaneously — court will need to reconcile.
4. **Timeline:** Case has been ongoing since 2019 (6+ years) with full resolution likely years away.
